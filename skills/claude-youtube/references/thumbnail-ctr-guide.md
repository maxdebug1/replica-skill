# Thumbnail and CTR guide

Thumbnails and titles as one unit, how to read CTR, and how to test.
Evidence levels as in `algorithm-guide.md`.

## CTR in context

- Impressions CTR counts clicks from thumbnail impressions on YouTube only
  (home, suggested, search, subscriptions and so on). External traffic is
  not in it [official].
- Half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR between
  2 and 10 percent [official]. That range is the only universal benchmark
  YouTube publishes.
- **CTR by niche** is not published by YouTube. Third-party "average CTR by
  niche" tables are built on small, unrepresentative samples; do not quote
  them as fact. The useful benchmark is the channel's own median for videos
  with similar impression counts and traffic mix. The patterns below are
  [observed]:
  - Search and notifications convert higher than Browse and Suggested.
  - Niche, high-intent topics (a specific software error, a specific repair)
    often show higher CTR on lower impressions; broad entertainment shows
    lower CTR on far more impressions.
  - CTR falls as a video reaches colder viewers. A falling CTR with rising
    impressions is the video succeeding.
- Never estimate CTR from views and subscriber counts. If the creator does
  not have Studio data to hand, say CTR is unknown.

## What makes a thumbnail work [rule of thumb]

1. **One focal point**, readable at about 160 px wide (the size on many
   phones). Squint at it.
2. **Contrast** in brightness and colour, against both YouTube's light and
   dark backgrounds and against the other thumbnails in the niche.
3. **Few words**, usually 0 to 3, that add to the title rather than repeat
   it.
4. **A reason to feel something**: curiosity, surprise, desire, concern,
   amusement.
5. **A curiosity gap**: show the situation or result, withhold the how.
6. **Consistency** loose enough that the channel is recognisable without
   every thumbnail looking the same.
7. **Bottom right is covered** by the duration stamp; keep it clear.

## Faces

- A face with a clear expression is one of the most reliable focal points:
  people notice faces and read emotion fast [observed]. The emotion should
  match the video's real tone.
- Faces are not required. Many channels in tutorials, tech, cooking, cars
  and finance do well with the object or the result as the focal point
  [observed]. Test it on the channel rather than assuming.
- Eye direction leads the viewer's eye; point it at the subject or text.
- Only use faces of people who agreed, and never a celebrity's face to imply
  an endorsement.

## Title and thumbnail pairing

The thumbnail grabs, the title explains. They should be two halves of one
idea, not the same idea twice.

| thumbnail shows | title says |
| --- | --- |
| a ruined cake next to a perfect one | "The one oven setting that fixed my baking" |
| a shocked face and a tiny apartment | "I lived in 90 sq ft for a month" |
| two products with a question mark | "Is the $40 one actually better?" |
| a result number circled in a chart | "How this tiny channel got 1M views" |

Title formulas: result, question, comparison, mistake, challenge or
experiment, numbered list, "how I", "why X", "the truth about X" (only if
the video delivers a real truth).

## A/B testing

- YouTube Studio's **Test and Compare** lets eligible channels test up to
  three thumbnails on a video (and, where available, titles), and picks a
  winner by watch time share rather than CTR alone [official]. Use it when
  available.
- Change one meaningful variable per test (face vs no face, text vs no text,
  close vs wide) so the result teaches something about the audience.
- Without the tool: swapping thumbnails manually confounds the result with
  the video's age and traffic changes. Treat it as weak evidence.
- Keep a simple log: video, variants, winner, margin, what it suggests.

## Policy [official]

Thumbnails must follow YouTube's Community Guidelines: no sexually
suggestive images to attract clicks, no graphic violence, no misleading
images that the video does not deliver.
